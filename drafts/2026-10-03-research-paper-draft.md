## The Question

As AI tools and systems rapidly progress, we are constantly hearing the same message: AI will let you do the same work with far less labor and it’s already happening especially in customer service jobs. This paper explores the question: is AI hitting customer service reps' jobs the way offshoring did (as a fall in the price of a substitute for human labor) and can we already see it in the numbers? The short answer is employment is down 11.1% from its 2019 peak, but it started falling before any company I could verify blamed AI, so these numbers cannot separate AI from offshoring-style cost pressure. I wrote this for a head of customer operations deciding whether to hold US customer service headcount, cut further, or bring jobs back onshore.

## Why It Is One Problem

Offshoring made answering service phone calls much cheaper. A rep overseas substituted US-based reps at a lower price. When the price of a substitutable input falls, firms shift toward it, and employment and pay in the pricier version fall. AI is the same disruption through another channel, since a chatbot or voice agent is another substitute whose cost per contact keeps dropping. The paper’s framework explores who is most exposed: scripted work like password resets and order status is easy to substitute, while exceptions and upset customers are not (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019; Autor et al., 2003).

## The Evidence

I used three data series and kept them separate: BLS employment and median pay for customer service representatives, May 2016 to May 2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics [BLS], 2026a); Indeed’s seasonally adjusted customer service postings index (Indeed Hiring Lab, 2026); and a dated log of companies blaming AI for customer support labor cuts. I wrote the tests down before pulling any data, so I could not pick whichever flattered the result. One limit up front of the data is that employment reports alone cannot tell an AI-driven drop from an offshoring-driven one, so only timing separates them. Also, BLS’s Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics data is a snapshot from May of each year so it can't show exactly when a change began.

**Did employment break from trend?** I drew a line through the 2016–2021 employment numbers and extended it to 2025 to show where employment was headed if nothing changed. Then I drew a floor below it as a cushion for normal ups and downs. If employment lands under the floor two years in a row, I consider it a break in the trend. I had two choices: the same cushion every year (a fixed band) or one that grows the further out I go (a forecast band). I picked the growing one because a line built on six data points is less trustworthy four years out. I ran both anyway (Appendix A), and they disagree. The fixed band puts 2024 and 2025 under the floor. The forecast band puts 2024 just above it and only 2025 below, by about 118,000 jobs, so the test did not detect a break. Still, employment clearly fell: 2025 is 11.1% below the 2019 peak and the lowest in the series (Figure 1).

**Did the decline start before firms blamed AI?** Job postings first fall two quarters in a row in 2022 Q2 at a 1% threshold and 2023 Q1 at my primary 3%. At the 5% threshold, postings never dropped fast enough two quarters in a row to meet it. Overall, postings fell about 41% from late 2021 to mid-2025, slowly and gradually, not as a steep drop. Employment’s first two straight declines start in 2023. For the company statements, I included a company only if it was talking about customer-facing work, publicly said AI caused the cut, and had a source and date I could verify. Only Salesforce cleared that. In early September 2025, its CEO said support headcount went from about 9,000 to 5,000 because of AI agents (TechRepublic, 2025). Uber (2026) is pending, and Hyatt (2025) does not count because it said its cut was unrelated to AI (Bloomberg News, 2026). Both declines start well before that statement, about two and a half years for postings and two years for employment, so AI may be a label on cuts already happening. If any AI-attributed cut is counted, C.H. Robinson (2022) and IBM (2023) would land right when postings began to fall (Ford, 2023; Founder Reports, n.d.; Appendix B). One possible reason is that some companies avoid naming AI as a cause of layoffs to protect their image. Hyatt may be an example: it said its cut was unrelated to AI, yet its AI lead says automation now helps cut its customer service costs (Bloomberg News, 2026).

Some surveys suggest there's more talk about AI cuts than actual cuts. Only 20% of customer service leaders had reduced agent headcount because of AI, and 55% reported stable staffing (Gartner, 2025). So, I kept the statements as a separate, non-numeric series: they record the stated cause, not the true internal one.

## What It Means and What to Do

So is AI the same disruption as offshoring? The tests show similar trends, but the data cannot confirm it yet. Two things would change that. The next BLS OEWS release will be the first to include the 2025–2026 cuts, and if it lands below the forecast floor, the break holds under either test. And if postings keep recovering, AI as a cause weakens.

My advice to a head of customer operations is to hold headcount rather than cut further or bring jobs back onshore. The tests give no case for a deep cut yet but do not wait for proof. To stay ahead of the curve, first map your team’s work by scripted work (password resets, order status) or judgment work (upset customers, issue anomalies, anything that needs human touch), then ease the scripted roles down through natural attrition and hiring freezes, not layoffs.

Second, repurpose the money you would have spent backfilling those roles into retraining your current reps. Every backfill you skip adds a set amount to a training budget. Retraining pays off slowly, with small gains in the first year that grow over two to three years (Card et al., 2018). Waiting for cuts to show up in your own numbers means delayed gains. Aim it at judgment work and at human-in-the-loop AI jobs like reviewing bot conversations. That work is harder, so it should pay more, or your best reps will leave. Real pay already fell 5.8% in 2022 (Appendix C).

Third, decide your warning signs ahead of time so headlines don't make the call for you. Watch the two outside signals above (the next BLS release and job postings), plus two of your own: how many customer contacts the AI resolves without a person, and how satisfied customers are. Decide now what readings mean speed up or pause, and tell your managers the plan up front.

Some may ask: the stricter test found no break, so why act at all? Because six data points can't tell us much, and not finding a break isn't proof there isn't one. A slow, reversible plan costs little if no break comes. And keeping a human option open covers the worry that customers typically hate talking to bots.
