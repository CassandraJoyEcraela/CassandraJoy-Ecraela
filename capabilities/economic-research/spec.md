# Spec: AI Automation & Customer Service Employment

**Course:** BUS620: Individual Research Paper **|** **Capability:** economic-research **|** **Status:** draft (pending approval & feedback from Professor Adam)

## Data Sources

- **BLS OEWS employment and wage data for customer service reps (SOC 43-4051)**: one set of numbers per year, 2016 through 2025 (the latest year BLS has published). This is the real, measured backbone of the paper: what actually happened to CSR jobs and pay across a stretch of time that covers both the offshoring years and the AI years. Each year's number is a snapshot taken in May, not an average across the whole year, and every chart built from this data notes that so the shading doesn't get mistaken for calendar years. On its own, this data can't tell us whether a drop in jobs came from offshoring or from AI: both would show up the same way, as fewer jobs and flat or falling pay. The only thing separating the two stories in this paper is timing, explained under Success Criteria.
- **Indeed Hiring Lab's Customer Service job postings index, via FRED (series ID: IHLIDXUSTPCUSTSERV)**: a daily count of CSR job postings, smoothed to remove normal seasonal hiring swings, and expressed as an index where February 1, 2020 is set to 100 and every other day is shown relative to it. Free to use as long as Indeed Hiring Lab is credited as the source. This is a leading indicator, a sign of hiring intent, tracked next to the BLS numbers rather than mixed in with them. Because the series starts on that same date, it covers the AI years (2022–2025) and the run-up to them, but doesn't reach back into the 2016–2021 offshoring-only baseline.
- **A timeline of company statements blaming AI for layoffs or hiring freezes**: built by collecting dated statements from earnings calls, press releases, and news coverage where a company names AI as the reason for cutting CSR jobs. This isn't a number-based series; it's kept separate from the two measured series above on purpose, so it can be checked against them rather than blended in and hidden.

## Model / Figures

- **Main figure: CSR employment, 2016–2025.** One line, one point per year. The line is split into an offshoring era (2016–2021) and an AI era (2022–2025), but that split is drawn from the break test's actual result, not from where the line just looks like it bends. The shading shows what the test found, not a guess. Caption reminds readers each point is a May snapshot.
- **Overlay: AI statements on the employment timeline.** Markers for each dated AI-attribution statement, placed on the same chart as the employment line, so you can see at a glance whether the statements show up before, during, or after any shift in employment.
- **Supporting figure: wages alongside employment.** A second line (or its own panel) tracking CSR wages over the same years, same May-snapshot note in the caption. If wages fall without employment falling, or the other way around, that's a different story than both dropping together, and this figure is what catches it.
- **Job postings vs. actual employment (2020–2025):** the Indeed Hiring Lab index plotted against real BLS employment numbers over the years they both cover, to see whether hiring intent (postings) moved before actual headcount did. Confirmed downloadable straight from FRED, no login needed. Limited to 2020–2025 for the reason above: the postings data doesn't go back any further.

## Success Criteria

A finished paper meets this spec if it:

- **Says what it found early, in plain terms.** A reader shouldn't have to reach the conclusion to find out whether the evidence says AI is acting like offshoring, or something different.
- **Makes the main figure stand on its own.** Someone who only looks at the employment chart, with the AI-statement markers, should be able to see the paper's claim without reading the surrounding text first.
- **Keeps what companies say separate from what the numbers show.** If there's a gap between the two (companies claiming one thing, the data showing another), that gap gets reported as a finding, not smoothed over or explained away.
- **Says plainly what it can't prove.** The BLS numbers alone can't tell an AI-driven decline apart from an offshoring-driven one; both would look the same. This paper only tells them apart by timing, and that's stated as a real limit of the design, not something extra data quietly fixes.
- **Owns the break test's limits, not just its outcome.** There are only six years of data (2016–2021) behind the test, so the margin it allows for normal variation could end up fairly wide. If 2022–2025 comes in inside that margin, the paper says "this test didn't catch a break," not "nothing happened." And the 90% cutoff is locked in before looking at 2022–2025, not adjusted afterward to fit whatever the data shows.
- **Answers a real decision.** The recommendation is written for a head of customer operations who has to decide whether to hold current CSR headcount, cut further, or bring jobs back onshore. Whatever the break test and timing test actually find determines which of those the paper recommends: the recommendation comes from the result, not the other way around.
- **Passes its own falsification test, defined as a number set in advance, not a judgment made after seeing the data:**
  - **Break test:** Fit a straight trend line to CSR employment from 2016–2021, the offshoring-only years, then project that line forward through 2025. Using how much the actual 2016–2021 numbers wobbled around that line, set a floor for 2022–2025: a level employment shouldn't fall below just from normal year-to-year noise, using a 90% cutoff. If 2022–2025 stays above that floor for at least two years running, that counts as a smooth continuation, with nothing new to explain. It takes two consecutive years below the floor to count as a real break, so a single odd year (like 2021's pandemic-reopening bounce) can't get mistaken for one on its own. Because the floor is built from only six data points, it might come out wide; the actual floor value and the actual 2022–2025 numbers are both reported in the text and the figure, and landing inside the floor is described as "the test didn't detect a break," never as "nothing happened."
  - **Timing test:** If job postings or employment start falling well before AI-attribution statements show up in the timeline, that's a sign AI might just be a label companies attach after the fact to cuts that were already happening for other reasons.
