# Research Brief: AI Automation & Customer Service Employment

**Course:** BUS620: Individual Research Paper **|** 
**Status:** draft (revised with Professor Adam's feedback)

## The Topic

US customer service jobs have been shrinking for two reasons: companies moving the work overseas, and companies replacing it with AI. This paper asks whether those are really the same story told twice (the cost of handling a customer's call drops, either because foreign labor is cheaper or because software is cheaper), or whether AI is doing something different enough from offshoring that it needs its own explanation.

This matters now because the two are hitting the same jobs at the same time. Offshoring has been eating into these jobs for decades, and companies have only started publicly blaming AI for cuts in this occupation since around 2022–2023.

## Research Question

How does AI-driven disruption compare to the long-running trend of US customer service jobs going overseas? Is AI hurting these jobs for the same basic reason offshoring did (a substitute for domestic workers getting cheaper), or is something different going on?

## Decision-Maker

This paper is written for a **head of customer operations** who has to decide whether to hold current US customer service headcount, cut further, or bring jobs back onshore. That decision depends on whether the 2022–2025 numbers show a real, lasting shift or just a temporary dip, and on whether AI is doing something new or just continuing a cost pressure offshoring already started.

## Economic Concepts

- **Labor as an input with substitutes:** US customer service workers aren't a company's only way to get customer calls handled. Foreign workers and AI software can both stand in for them. If either gets cheaper, companies use fewer US workers. So if AI and offshoring are really the same force, AI job losses should look a lot like offshoring job losses.
- **Structural vs. cyclical displacement:** telling permanent job loss apart from temporary loss that bounces back. A pandemic dip that recovers is cyclical. A decline that never comes back because the need for the job is gone is structural. If I mix these up, a temporary blip could get mistaken for a lasting AI effect, or the other way around.

## Data Sources

- **BLS OEWS data for customer service reps (SOC 43-4051):** 10 years of published annual data (2016–2025). This is the main measured data: what actually happened to jobs and pay, not an article about it. On its own it can't tell an AI-driven decline from an offshoring-driven one, because both would look the same. So in this paper the two are separated by timing, not by measuring each one directly. Each year blends three years of survey responses, which blurs the timing of any change, so I flag that as a limit.
- **Indeed Hiring Lab's Customer Service job postings index, from FRED:** a hint of hiring plans, tracked next to the BLS data, not mixed into it. It starts in February 2020, so it covers the AI years but not the full 2016–2025 stretch.
- **CPI-U, from FRED:** used only to turn wages into inflation-adjusted dollars.
- **A timeline of companies blaming AI for layoffs or hiring freezes:** kept separate so I can compare it to the measured data instead of blending it in. Only statements from the company itself that name customer-facing service roles set the clock in the timing check, under a rule I wrote down before collecting anything. Statements about tech support and IT roles are logged separately and used only as a backup check.

## Planned Analysis

I'll compare US customer service employment and wages from 2016 to 2025, which covers both the offshoring years and the AI years, and check whether 2022–2025 breaks from the earlier trend. I set the cutoff for a "break" in advance, so I'm not deciding by eye whether the line looks different. The trend is built from all of 2016–2021, including the two pandemic years. That adds noise and can make the cutoff wider, but dropping those years would leave too little data to judge what normal ups and downs look like, and I'll say so in the paper. If the numbers stay inside the cutoff, I'll report that as "the test didn't detect a break," not as proof that nothing changed.

Wages are shown in inflation-adjusted dollars, because prices rose a lot over this stretch and raw dollars would make pay look like it was climbing when much of that is just inflation. The raw figures stay alongside, and I chose this approach before pulling any data.

Since a decline in this data fits either offshoring or AI, I can't tell them apart from employment numbers alone, so I check timing separately. I look at whether job postings or employment started falling well before the first company statement blaming AI for customer service cuts: at least two quarters before for postings, at least a year before for employment. A postings quarter counts as falling if it drops at least 3% from the quarter before, and I also try 1% and 5% to see if the answer depends on that number. Any fall that starts in 2020 or 2021 is ignored, so the pandemic doesn't get mistaken for an early AI effect. A fall that starts closer to the first statement than that gets reported as inconclusive. As one more check, I'll see whether the answer changes if the earliest tech support or IT statement sets the clock instead, since those jobs fall outside the BLS occupation I'm measuring.
