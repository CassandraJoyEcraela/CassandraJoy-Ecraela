# Research Brief: AI Automation & Customer Service Employment

**Course:** BUS620 — Individual Research Paper
**Status:** Pending feedback by Professor Adam (initial topic approved)

## The Topic

Customer service representative (CSR) jobs have been shrinking for two reasons: companies moving CSR work overseas, and companies replacing CSR work with AI. This paper tests whether those are actually the same story told twice — a fall in the cost of a unit of customer service labor, delivered through two different channels (cheaper foreign labor vs. cheaper software) — or whether the AI disruption behaves differently enough from offshoring to need its own explanation.

This matters now because the two channels are colliding in the same occupation at the same time: offshoring has been eroding CSR employment for decades and AI-attributed layoffs in the same occupation have only become common in company statements since roughly 2022–2023.

## Research Question

How does AI-driven disruption compare to the long-standing trend of US-based customer service jobs being outsourced overseas? Is AI hurting domestic customer service jobs for the same basic reason offshoring did (a fall in the price of a substitutable labor input), or is something different happening?

## Economic Concepts

- **Labor as an input with substitutes** — US-based CSR labor isn't a company's only way to get customer calls handled; foreign workers and AI software are both stand-ins for it. If either stand-in gets cheaper, companies use less domestic CSR labor for it — so AI-driven job loss should look like offshoring-driven job loss if they're really the same force at work.
- **Elasticity of labor demand** — how much CSR hiring actually shrinks when a cheaper stand-in becomes available. Some jobs barely move even when a substitute gets much cheaper; others shrink fast. This measures how touchy CSR employment is to that price drop.
- **Structural vs. cyclical displacement** — telling apart a job loss that's permanent from one that's temporary and bounces back. A COVID-era dip that later recovers is cyclical; a decline that never comes back because the underlying need for the job disappeared is structural. Without separating these, a temporary blip could get mistaken for a lasting AI effect, or the reverse.

## Data Sources

- **BLS OEWS employment and wage data, SOC 43-4051 (Customer Service Representatives)** — the most recent 10 years of published annual data. This is the core measured series: what actually happened to CSR jobs and pay, not an article describing it.
- **LinkedIn job postings data for CSR roles, by year** — a leading indicator of hiring intent, tracked alongside the BLS series rather than blended into it.
- **A timeline of company statements attributing layoffs or hiring freezes to AI** — kept as a separate, non-numeric series, so it can be compared against the two measured series above rather than folded into them.

## Planned Analysis

I'll compare US-based CSR employment and wages from 2016 to 2025, a window that covers both the offshoring era and the AI era, to see whether the AI years look like a continuation of the existing trend or a real break from it. Then, separately, I'll check the timing: did company statements blaming AI for cuts come out around the same time employment actually shifted, or did the AI story show up before or after the numbers moved?
