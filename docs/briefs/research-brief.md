# Research Brief: AI Automation & Customer Service Employment

**Course:** BUS620: Individual Research Paper **|** 
**Status:** Pending feedback by Professor Adam (initial topic approved)

## The Topic

US-based customer service representative (CSR) jobs have been shrinking for two reasons: companies moving the work overseas, and companies replacing it with AI. This paper asks whether those are really the same story told twice (a drop in what it costs to get a customer's call handled, arriving through two different channels: cheaper foreign labor vs. cheaper software), or whether AI is doing something different enough from offshoring that it needs its own explanation.

This matters right now because the two are colliding in the same jobs at the same time: offshoring has been eating into CSR employment for decades, and companies have only started publicly blaming AI for cuts in this same occupation since around 2022–2023.

## Research Question

How does AI-driven disruption compare to the long-running trend of US customer service jobs being sent overseas? Is AI hurting domestic CSR jobs for the same basic reason offshoring did (a substitute for domestic labor getting cheaper), or is something different going on?

## Decision-Maker

This paper is written for a **head of customer operations** who has to decide whether to hold current domestic CSR headcount, cut further, or bring jobs back onshore. That decision hinges on whether the 2022–2025 numbers show a real, lasting shift or just a temporary dip, and on whether AI is genuinely doing something new or just continuing a cost pressure offshoring already started.

## Economic Concepts

- **Labor as an input with substitutes**: US-based CSR labor isn't a company's only way to get customer calls handled; foreign workers and AI software can both stand in for it. If either stand-in gets cheaper, companies use less domestic CSR labor. So if AI and offshoring are really the same force at work, AI-driven job loss should look a lot like offshoring-driven job loss.
- **Structural vs. cyclical displacement**: telling a permanent job loss apart from a temporary one that bounces back. A COVID-era dip that later recovers is cyclical; a decline that never comes back because the need for the job is gone for good is structural. Mixing these up means a temporary blip could get mistaken for a lasting AI effect, or the other way around.

## Data Sources

- **BLS OEWS employment and wage data for customer service reps (SOC 43-4051)**: the most recent 10 years of published annual data (2016–2025). This is the real, measured backbone: what actually happened to CSR jobs and pay, not an article describing it. On its own, it can't tell an AI-driven decline apart from an offshoring-driven one; both would look the same. So in this paper, the two are told apart by timing, not by measuring each channel separately.
- **Indeed Hiring Lab's Customer Service job postings index, via FRED**: a sign of hiring intent, tracked next to the BLS numbers rather than folded into them. It starts in February 2020, so it covers the AI years but not the full 2016–2025 offshoring-era stretch.
- **A timeline of company statements blaming AI for layoffs or hiring freezes**: kept as its own, separate track, so it can be checked against the two measured series above instead of blended into them.

## Planned Analysis

I'll compare US CSR employment and wages from 2016 to 2025 (a window covering both the offshoring years and the AI years) and check whether 2022–2025 breaks from the earlier trend using a cutoff I set in advance, rather than deciding by eye whether the line looks different. Because that cutoff is built from only six years of pre-AI data, it might be too loose to catch a real but modest decline, so if the numbers clear it, I'll report that as "this test didn't catch a break," not as proof nothing changed. And because a decline in this data is consistent with either offshoring or AI, I can't tell the two apart from the employment numbers alone, so I'll check timing separately, comparing whether company statements blaming AI show up before, during, or after the employment and postings numbers actually move.
